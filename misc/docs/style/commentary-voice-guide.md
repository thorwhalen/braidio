# The Commentary-Voice Guide

*Master reference for writing (and prompting for) the host voice of a music-and-history podcast.*

This guide exists because the show has a **voice**, and a voice is a set of choices — not a template. Everything below is in service of one discipline: **replace rhetorical scaffolding with cashable particulars.** Every general claim should be redeemable in a specific line, chord, cut, or beat you can point to. When it can't be, the sentence is empty no matter how good it sounds.

Read it in three passes:
1. **Section 1** — the traps *we specifically* keep falling into. Fix these first; they are the difference between the show sounding alive and sounding like a mold being refilled.
2. **Section 2** — the axes, a shared vocabulary for describing and tuning any voice.
3. **Section 3** — Routing: how to choose and blend the reference voices for a given episode.
4. **Section 4** — the index of per-voice files and style groups.

---

## 1. Avoid Rhetorical Emptiness — our actual overused tics

We have a small, tightly-recycled kit of moves. Individually most are good instincts — they're what a sharp host does. The problem is **exclusivity and frequency**: because the same four or five gestures carry every insight, the analysis starts to feel like a template being refilled rather than a mind reacting to *this* song. The fix in almost every case is the same principle: **reserve the move for when it's genuinely earned, vary the delivery, and more often just STATE the insight and trust the listener to feel it** instead of narrating that a moment is significant.

Below are thirteen named patterns. For each: what it is, why it deadens on repeat, and the concrete change.

### 1. The Director's Cue — *"Listen to / notice / watch / catch how X"*
> *"Listen to what she does to one word: mine." · "Catch the shift in the music." · "Count the rhymes."*

**Why it deadens:** It's the single most overused move in the corpus — 30+ beats open or pivot on it — so it stops functioning as emphasis and becomes verbal throat-clearing. Worse, it does contradictory work: you order the listener to notice something and then notice it *for* them in the same breath, so their attention is narrated, never engaged.
**Fix:** Default to just making the observation. *"Her certainty dissolves across one word: mine"* lands harder than *"Listen to what she does to one word."* Ration the explicit cue to the two or three moments per episode where you want the listener to hear an audio detail **before** you interpret it — and there, let the clip actually play in the gap. Never cue something you're about to explain anyway.

### 2. The Chiasmus Flip — *"the X that did good Y is the X that does terrible Z"*
> *"The pen that delivered the boy is the pen that buries the man." · "He saved his honor by burning down his home."*

**Why it deadens:** A great figure the *first* time. But it's now the house style for every reversal, so the listener hears the flip coming a clause early — killing the surprise that is the whole point of a chiasmus. The symmetry also flatters history into a bumper sticker, as if tragedy obligingly arranged itself into an epigram. ("Pen that delivered / pen that buries" literally recurs across two episodes.)
**Fix:** Cap it at ~one per episode; never let the same noun (pen, honor) anchor the flip in adjacent episodes. When you feel the flip coming, hold the two facts side by side *without* the mirror and let the listener assemble the irony: *"At seventeen the letter bought him a ship off the island. At forty the pamphlet cost him the presidency. Same instinct."* The asymmetry of real prose out-convinces the symmetry of an aphorism.

### 3. The Negation-Escalation — *"It's not A — it's B"*
> *"He isn't the victim here — he's the witness for the prosecution against himself." · "the affair isn't what destroys him — the cover-up is."*

**Why it deadens:** The not-X-but-Y frame manufactures a false correction — it sweeps away a wrong reading nobody held, so B arrives dressed as revelation when it's often just the plain point. Used constantly, every observation becomes a fake plot twist and the listener feels *handled*.
**Fix:** Only negate when there's a **real** competing reading worth demolishing (the "room where it happens is actually about *not* being in the room" earns it — the surface is genuinely misleading). Otherwise state B directly. Test: delete *"It's not A —"* and if the sentence is just as clear, A was a straw man — cut it.

### 4. The Reduction Formula — *"That's the whole marriage in one line"*
> *"That's the whole marriage in one line." · "the thesis of the whole character in seven words." · "the scandal in one line."*

**Why it deadens:** It claims a compression the surrounding paragraph then contradicts by spending five more sentences explaining. If a line really held "the whole marriage," you wouldn't need the gloss. Repeated, it reads as the host congratulating the material (and himself) for being deep rather than demonstrating depth. "Whole" is doing inflationary lifting it can't cash.
**Fix:** Show the compression, don't assert it. Quote the line, land ONE precise consequence — *"'Eliza, I've got so much on my plate.' He answers her love with a to-do list."* Drop the label; if the juxtaposition works, the listener supplies "the whole marriage," and that click is the payoff you're currently spending on narration.

### 5. The Portentous Closer — the mandatory grand aphorism to end every episode
> *"The last thing this song leaves us isn't the fire. It's what the fire couldn't touch." · "The code of honor took the boy. Love kept the name."*

**Why it deadens:** Because **every** episode does it, the ending stops being a peak and becomes a formatting requirement — the listener learns the last twenty seconds will always be a hushed, symmetrical epigram, so the gravity self-discounts. Several reach for portent the material doesn't support, straining a clean historical point into a cosmic koan. The final-fragment cadence (*short. short. shorter.*) becomes a tic you can set your watch by.
**Fix:** Vary the exit deliberately across the series — sometimes a hard concrete fact, sometimes an unresolved question, sometimes the last clip with barely a word after. When you *do* write an aphoristic close, make sure it says something the episode didn't already say five times — it should crystallize, not re-perfume. Kill the reflexive terminal fragment; a plain declarative closes with more authority.

### 6. The "Here's X" Handoff — the universal connective
> *"Here's a feeling you know:" · "Here's the cruelest part of the deal." · "Here's Ron Chernow on…"*

**Why it deadens:** "Here's" is the most frequent transition in the corpus — it prefaces passages, insights, feelings, and asides indiscriminately, giving the prose a monotone *block → "here's" → block* rhythm. It also front-loads a promise ("here's the astonishing part") the payoff then has to live up to; when it's merely interesting, the deflation is on you.
**Fix:** Audit every "here's" and cut or vary at least two-thirds. For book hand-offs, rotate the framing — attribute by what Chernow *does* (*"Chernow lingers on how much he chose to include"*), or quote him cold with no runway. Save "here's the astonishing part" for something you'd stake the episode on.

### 7. Naming the Machinery — *"the turn," "the tell," "the button," "the trick"*
> *"Then the turn — and notice what Eliza reaches for first." · "and that's the tell." · "here's his trick: he never once lets you into the room."*

**Why it deadens:** Insider craft-vocabulary leaking into the product. Calling a moment "the tell" explains the mechanism instead of delivering its effect — like a magician announcing "and here's the misdirection." Repeated, the host sounds like he's *grading* the songwriting rather than being moved by it, and distinct emotional moments flatten into the same three technical labels.
**Fix:** Trust the move to work unlabeled. Instead of *"that's the tell — she flatters his reputation,"* just: *"She doesn't seduce the man. She flatters the reputation."* The listener feels the tell without the word. Reserve craft-vocabulary for the rare beat where the craft itself is the subject (Miranda's structural choices) — and even then, once.

### 8. The One-Word / Two-Meanings Spotlight — announced as such
> *"Two meanings of one word, back to back. First… Then the turn…" · the 'far as I could see' → 'far as I can see' pronoun dwell.*

**Why it deadens:** A genuinely sharp close-reading habit that's become reflexive — and when the double meaning is thin, the machinery shows. Announcing "two meanings of one word" pre-explains the effect you're about to demonstrate, deflating it. Several episodes *hunt* for a load-bearing word whether or not the song pivots on one, manufacturing significance rather than finding it.
**Fix:** Keep the close reading; stop pre-announcing it. Place the two uses next to each other and pause — don't caption them "meaning one, meaning two." Only deploy where the word genuinely carries the double load (*burn*, *mine* both earn it). Sometimes the insight is a whole phrase or an absence, not a single word — don't force the spotlight every episode.

### 9. The Immersive Command — *"Picture the room / sit in their chairs"*
> *"So picture it: the Treasury Secretary…" · "Picture the room." · "so sit in their chairs: secret cash flowing…"*

**Why it deadens:** A stagey second-person command that tells the listener to do imaginative work instead of writing prose vivid enough to make them do it involuntarily. "Picture the room" is a confession that the next sentence won't conjure the room on its own. Twice in one episode, it becomes a nervous tic.
**Fix:** Replace the command with the concrete detail that *makes* them picture it: *"Three men climb the stairs to Hamilton's office, certain they hold the paper that ends him"* — the preceding "Picture the room" is dead weight. Let sensory specifics do the immersion; if the detail is strong, you never have to ask.

### 10. Fragment-Stacking for Gravity — clipped noun-phrases in a row
> *"Haggard. Neglected. Pacing for half an hour." · "Two ruined wives." · "Nobody's clean. Nobody tells. The rumor just grows."*

**Why it deadens:** The staccato fragment is our default gravity-delivery system, and because it's everywhere the rhythm *itself* signals "be moved now" — exactly the instruction that makes a listener resist. When real sentences never vary into a fragment, the fragment shocks; when every solemn beat is one, the device wears a groove.
**Fix:** Treat the fragment as spice, not base. Convert most back into full sentences and let ONE fragment per episode carry the weight it's built for. Watch the terminal three-fragment cadence especially (*"Nobody's clean. Nobody tells. The rumor grows."*) — our most repeated rhythm. Break it by ending on a full clause sometimes.

### 11. The Attribution Flourish — *"his word, not mine" / "Chernow calls it X"*
> *"One time becomes a pastime — his word, not mine." · "Chernow calls it a genius for the self-inflicted wound."*

**Why it deadens:** "His word, not mine" is a wink that says *notice I'm being clever with sourcing* — charming once, mannered by the third time. Leaning on "Chernow calls it X" for the episode's key phrase outsources the host's own voice: the sharpest formulations keep belonging to the book, so the host reads as a highlighter rather than a thinker.
**Fix:** Use the borrowed phrase without the meta-flag — *"One time becomes a pastime"* is stronger without the tag hanging off it; if attribution matters, work it in cleanly earlier. Make sure at least the **central** insight of each episode is in the host's own words: let the book supply evidence, you supply the formulation.

### 12. The Identity-Echo — *"Same man, same gift / same instinct"*
> *"Same man, same gift: the instinct that at seventeen turned a hurricane into a ticket…" · "Same instinct: win the argument, lose everything else."*

**Why it deadens:** A thesis-welding phrase that does the interpretive work by fiat — *asserting* continuity rather than demonstrating it, in the same two-word cadence each time. Because the whole series rides on "Hamilton's gift is also his ruin," hammering "same man, same gift" makes the show feel like it has exactly one idea it keeps restating with new songs plugged in.
**Fix:** If you've laid the two events side by side well, the sameness is the inference the listener *draws* — handing it to them steals their conclusion. When you must connect explicitly, vary the language, and more importantly **complicate the through-line**: find the episode where "gift-becomes-ruin" *doesn't* fit cleanly, so the series has more than one gear.

### 13. The Callback Signpost — *"Earlier X / Now Y"*
> *"Earlier… this same Eliza sang, 'Let me be a part of the narrative.' Now she doesn't ask." · "the exact words of a song he sang as a nine-year-old."*

**Why it deadens:** The callback-spotting is real value — but the delivery is always the same explicit "Earlier X, now Y" scaffold, so a genuinely delightful discovery arrives in the flat cadence of a footnote. "This same Eliza" / "the exact words of" makes every callback sound like the host checking a box labeled *reprise*.
**Fix:** Keep finding the callbacks — some of the best material — but vary the reveal. Sometimes drop the earlier line **without** flagging it and let listeners who know the show catch it. Sometimes lead with the echo and let the contrast bloom slowly. Sometimes play both clips back-to-back and say almost nothing. The insight is strong enough that it doesn't need the training wheels every time.

### The one rule underneath all thirteen
> **When a move is doing the feeling *for* the listener, cut it and let the particular do the feeling *in* the listener.** Narrating that a moment is significant is the opposite of making it significant.

## 2. The Style Axes — a working vocabulary

Twelve axes describe the design space of a spoken critical voice. Use them two ways: to **describe** a reference voice ("Bragg is high on Authority-settled, low on Density"), and to **tune** a draft ("this beat is flat — vary the Sentence Music, drop the Density"). Two axes do the most to separate real criticism from filler in spoken form: **Handling of Evidence** and **Sentence Music**. Weight them.

| Axis | Spectrum | Use it to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Register** | scholarly/technical ← literary ← plain conversational ← vernacular/slang | Am I modulating, or stuck in one band? Earn a technical term by setting it next to a plain one; drop into slang *because* the surrounding prose is careful. A fixed register is usually a tell. |
| **Distance / Intimacy** | detached verdict ↔ "we"-inclusive tour-guide ↔ confiding first-person | How close am I standing? Intimacy raises stakes and risk; distance buys authority. Intimacy can be exacting; detachment can be lazy. |
| **Structure** | thesis→evidence→conclusion ↔ braided theme-and-variation ↔ associative/digressive ↔ single unspooling | Does the listener need a map or a current? Spoken form rewards a shape the ear can follow without a visible skeleton. |
| **Stance / Verdict** | advocacy ↔ analytic neutrality ↔ skeptical interrogation ↔ polemic | Is the judgment *discovered* mid-close-reading, or announced up front and then defended? The cheap version is contrarianism; the earned version makes the verdict fall out of the observation. |
| **Humor & Irony** | earnest ↔ dry wit ↔ playful irony ↔ arch snark | Does this joke *carry* an observation or *replace* one? Sincerity is the harder, riskier, often more powerful move in speech — a laugh can be a dodge. |
| **Density / Allusiveness** | spare/plain ↔ balanced ↔ rich/allusive ↔ baroque | For the ear, err spare. Let the rare dense sentence spike *because* it's surrounded by air. Print density drowns a listener. |
| **Sentence Music** | clipped/fragmentary ↔ varied/syncopated ↔ flowing/periodic ↔ incantatory | Am I varying length? A short sentence lands hardest right after a long one. The default LLM cadence — three medium clauses, comma, summarizing coda, repeat — is the enemy. **This is the primary instrument.** |
| **Use of the Personal** | impersonal/no-I ↔ occasional first-person reaction ↔ memoir-braided ↔ confessional | Is my "I" *evidence* (a specific reaction that reveals the work) or throat-clearing autobiography? Spend the personal where a lived reaction illuminates the song. |
| **Handling of Evidence** | close reading / direct citation ↔ paraphrase-plus-example ↔ assertion-with-gesture ↔ pure pronouncement | **The axis that most separates criticism from filler.** Can I point to the actual line/chord/cut? Evaluative adjectives are IOUs the close reading has to pay. |
| **Authority Posture** | authoritative/settled ↔ guiding but open ↔ thinking-aloud ↔ openly uncertain | Am I transmitting a settled verdict or working it out on-mic? Provisionality is a *feature* in speech; "I can't fully account for why this bridge works" often buys more trust than certainty. |
| **Tempo / Dwelling** | brisk/panoramic ↔ steady ↔ dwelling/microscopic | Am I covering or dwelling? Depth comes from spending more time on less. Pick two or three moments worth stopping the clock for. Coverage is the enemy of insight. |
| **Figuration** | literal/mechanical ↔ occasional clarifying analogy ↔ metaphor-forward ↔ conceit-driven | Does this figure do work the literal statement can't? Keep it only if removing it loses *meaning*, not just flavor. Ornament is the first refuge of an empty sentence. |

**How to use the axes in practice.** A voice is a *position* on these axes plus a *modulation pattern* across them. When you route (Section 3), you're really choosing a target position; when you revise, you're checking a draft against that position and — crucially — checking that **Sentence Music varies** and **every claim on the Evidence axis is cashable.**

## 3. Routing — picking and mixing voices for a production

The reference files (Section 4) are not costumes to put on whole. Each one is a **bundle of two things**:

1. **A writing signature** — cadence, diction, structure, humor, the shape of the sentences.
2. **A research lens** — *what the host pays attention to*, what questions they ask of a song, what counts as the interesting thing.

**These are inseparable.** Borrowing McWhorter's voice without his "treat the song as evidence, trace who borrowed what from whom" attention gets you a mannerism. Borrowing his attention without his relaxed second-person warmth gets you a lecture. **Always import the pair: how the host writes AND what the host notices.**

### The core method: blend 2–4, never impersonate 1

A single reference voice, copied whole, produces pastiche — and worse, it locks you into that voice's failure mode (see each file's *what-to-avoid*). Instead, build each production from a **blend of two to four voices**, assigned to distinct jobs.

A workable blend has:

- **One or two DRIVER voices** — the dominant cadence and the primary research lens. This is who the episode *sounds* like and *thinks* like most of the time.
- **One TEXTURE voice** — a secondary attention that keeps the driver honest: a different thing to notice, a different question. It shows up in specific beats, not throughout.
- **One CHECK voice** — a corrective posture that guards against the driver's excess: skepticism against enthusiasm, concreteness against abstraction, silence against over-narration.

Assign them explicitly. Example blends (illustrative, not prescriptive):

- **A song's craft + its history** → Driver: **Sloan & Harding** (earn-the-term, craft-to-culture bridge) + **McWhorter** (hidden mechanism, who-borrowed-from-whom). Texture: **Hirway** (disassemble a component, let the listener hear it solo, restore the whole). Check: **Louis Theroux** (withhold the verdict; let a detail or an unresolved question sit instead of tying a bow).
- **A biographical / historical episode** → Driver: **Bragg** (chronological spine, define terms, "why does this still matter") + **Barbaro** (earn the news, open on one scene, restate-to-clarify). Texture: **Longworth** (weigh conflicting sources, name what can't be known). Check: **Amanda Dobbins**-style audience-surrogate skepticism (does this actually land on a normal listener, or are we overclaiming?).
- **An emotional deep-dive on a beloved song** → Driver: **Mallory Rubin** (thematic close-reading as literature, accumulation, earned earnestness). Texture: **Katy Hessel** (lead with the human story and the first-encounter charge). Check: **John McWhorter** / **Jon Caramanica** (empirical specificity — name the exact device, describe the texture in fresh language, so the feeling is grounded not gushed).

### Rules for mixing

1. **Name the job before the voice.** Decide what the episode needs — a spine, a lens, a corrective — *then* pick the voice that supplies it. Casting first, story second, produces a variety show.
2. **The Check voice is non-negotiable.** Our tics (Section 1) are all excesses of enthusiasm and craft-display. Every blend needs at least one voice whose *whole value* is restraint — Theroux's withheld verdict, Dobbins's skepticism, Hirway's self-erasure, Bragg's momentum-cut. It's the built-in antidote to our house failure modes.
3. **Blend attention, not just cadence.** The point of a second voice is a second *thing to notice*. If both your voices notice the same thing, you have one voice with two accents. Pick voices whose research lenses genuinely differ (craft vs. commerce; process vs. biography; the human vs. the mechanism).
4. **Let one voice own the exit.** Section 1's Portentous Closer is our worst tic. Rotate which reference *shapes the ending* across the series — a Bragg "why it matters," a Theroux unresolved question, a Hirway "just play the finished thing," a hard Caramanica fact. The ending should not always sound like the same person.
5. **Ration the loud voices.** The comedy-deconstruction and maximalist voices (Mantzoukas, Salek, Newman) are seasoning at most — a texture beat, never a driver, for a warm history show. Their charisma is load-bearing and inimitable; borrow their *method* (fixate on one telling detail; treat a trivial thing as worthy of total attention) not their volume.
6. **Match intimacy to material.** Solemnity suits the tragic beats; delight suits the craft beats. Barbaro's gravitas over a joyful discovery reads as funereal; Theroux's flat non-judgment where a real point of view is wanted reads as evasive. Move along the Distance and Humor axes to fit the beat, not the whole episode.
7. **Write the blend into the prompt.** When briefing a writer or a model, state it as: *"Driver = X (for its Y lens + Z cadence); Texture = A on the [specific beat]; Check = B to prevent [our tic]. Ending owned by C this week."* That single line does more than any adjective.

## 4. Index — reference files and style groups

### Per-voice reference files

Each file carries a full profile: domain, writing-style, research-focus, signature moves, what-to-borrow, what-to-avoid, and a cadence note. Pull the pair (writing + attention) when you route.

| Voice | File | Primary use in the blend |
|---|---|---|
| **Mallory Rubin** | `mallory-rubin` | Earnest thematic close-reading as literature; accumulation to an earned emotional peak; ritual scaffold + signature close. |
| **Michael Barbaro** | `michael-barbaro` | Narrative-interview instincts: earn the news, open on one scene, restate-to-clarify, the short propulsive question. |
| **Melvyn Bragg** | `melvyn-bragg` | Erudite generalist spine: chronology + biography, define the term, brisk momentum, "why does this still matter." |
| **Louis Theroux** | `louis-theroux` | Immersive-doc restraint: the naive question, withheld verdict, the telling small detail, self-implicating warmth. |
| **John McWhorter** | `john-mcwhorter` | Song-as-evidence: hidden mechanism, cultural hybridity, "you'd think X but actually Y," delight as authority. |
| **Hrishikesh Hirway** | `hrishikesh-hirway` | Disassemble → hear the part solo → surface the human decision → reassemble; process over biography; get out of the way. |
| **Nate Sloan & Charlie Harding** | `nate-sloan-charlie-harding` | Earn-the-jargon; demonstrate don't describe; craft-to-culture bridge; take "disposable" music seriously. |
| **Jon Caramanica** | `jon-caramanica` | Texture obsession — describe exactly what a sound/voice feels like in fresh language; the earned contrarian reframe. |
| **Karina Longworth** | `karina-longworth` | Scripted narrative history; source-consciousness worn lightly; recovering the overlooked; voice as immersion. |
| **Katy Hessel** | `katy-hessel` | Warm "look at this with me" on-ramp; lead with the human story and reason-for-obscurity; first-encounter charge. |
| **Helen Molesworth** | `helen-molesworth` | The open question as method; theory-plus-feeling; find the labor/social relation inside a work read purely formally. |

### Style groups (the fallback file: `groups_md`)

When no single per-voice file fits, or when you want the *domain posture* rather than one person's mannerisms, route by group. The groups file describes seven domain signatures — each with its writing-style and research-focus — and folds in the secondary voices who don't have standalone files (the *Big Picture*, *Blank Check*, *How Did This Get Made?*, and *Talk Art* players, plus Yasi Salek):

- **music-analysis** — craft/mechanism first, then meaning *(Hirway, Sloan & Harding, McWhorter, Caramanica)*
- **narrative-journalism** — story, chronology, earn-the-news *(Barbaro)*
- **erudite-roundtable** — generalist spine, define terms, auteur-career throughlines *(Bragg; folds in Sims, Newman)*
- **immersive-doc** — telling detail, withheld verdict, source-consciousness *(Theroux, Longworth)*
- **culture-criticism** — subjective reaction owned and justified; commerce and fandom as lenses *(Rubin, Caramanica; folds in Fennessey, Dobbins, Salek)*
- **comedy-deconstruction** — fixate on one detail, ground absurdity in real context *(folds in Scheer, Raphael, Mantzoukas)*
- **art-conversation** — democratizing warmth, the human origin story, the generative question *(Hessel, Molesworth; folds in Tovey & Diament)*

Use a group when you want a *stance* and a *research lens* without importing a specific person's tics — and always cross-check the result against Section 1.
