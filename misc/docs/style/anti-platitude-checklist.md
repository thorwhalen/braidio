# Anti-platitude checklist (paste into writer prompts)

Crisp DO-NOTs distilled from the guide — drop straight into a writing-agent prompt.

1. DO NOT open or pivot a beat with 'Listen to / notice / watch / catch how…' — just make the observation. Reserve an explicit audio cue for the 2–3 moments per episode where the clip plays BEFORE you interpret it, and never for something you're about to explain anyway.
2. DO NOT use the chiasmus flip ('the X that did Y is the X that does Z') more than once per episode, and never let the same noun anchor it in adjacent episodes — hold the two facts side by side without the mirror and let the listener assemble the irony.
3. DO NOT frame a point as 'It's not A — it's B' unless A is a reading someone actually holds. If deleting 'It's not A —' leaves the sentence just as clear, A is a straw man: cut it and state B directly.
4. DO NOT assert compression ('that's the whole marriage in one line,' 'the thesis in seven words'). Quote the line, land ONE precise consequence, and let the listener supply the 'whole' — that click is the payoff.
5. DO NOT end every episode on a hushed symmetrical aphorism or a terminal sentence fragment. Rotate exits across the series — a hard concrete fact, an unresolved question, the last clip with almost no words — and only crystallize something the episode hasn't already said.
6. DO NOT use 'Here's…' as a default connective. Audit and cut or vary at least two-thirds; introduce book passages by what the author DOES, or quote cold with no runway.
7. DO NOT name the rhetorical machinery out loud ('the turn,' 'the tell,' 'the button,' 'his trick'). Deliver the effect instead of labeling the mechanism; the listener feels the tell without the word.
8. DO NOT pre-announce a close reading ('two meanings of one word, back to back'). Place the two uses next to each other and pause. Only spotlight a single word when it genuinely carries the double load — don't manufacture one every episode.
9. DO NOT issue immersion commands ('Picture the room,' 'sit in their chairs'). Write the concrete sensory detail that makes the listener picture it involuntarily; if the detail is strong you never have to ask.
10. DO NOT default to stacked sentence fragments for gravity — especially the terminal three-fragment cadence. Treat the fragment as spice: one per episode, surrounded by full sentences, so it actually spikes.
11. DO NOT flag borrowed phrases with 'his word, not mine' or lean on 'Chernow calls it X' for your key formulation. Use the borrowed phrase clean, and make sure each episode's central insight is in your own words — the book supplies evidence, you supply the formulation.
12. DO NOT weld two moments together by asserting 'same man, same gift / same instinct.' Lay the events side by side and let the sameness be the listener's inference — and actively hunt for the case where the through-line DOESN'T fit cleanly.
13. DO NOT deliver every callback in the flat 'Earlier X, now Y' scaffold. Vary the reveal — sometimes drop the earlier line unflagged, sometimes play both clips back-to-back and say almost nothing.
14. DO NOT spend an evaluative adjective ('brilliant,' 'masterful,' 'devastating,' 'stunning') where you can point to the exact line, chord, cut, or beat that provoked it. If you can't point to the particular, you don't have the observation yet — you have a placeholder.
15. DO NOT let any move do the feeling FOR the listener. Show the mechanism, undersell rather than over-italicize, cut the pre-chewed takeaway, and stop one sentence earlier than feels safe.
