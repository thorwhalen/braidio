# Sean Fennessey
*Film / movie-industry commentary*
**Shows:** The Big Picture (The Ringer); formerly Grantland, editor roles at The Ringer

## How they write / speak
Conversational-authoritative. Speaks in long, confident, well-organized paragraphs that sound like a lede written out loud — he'll set up a thesis, qualify it, and land a verdict. Warm but declarative; comfortable pronouncing something 'the best' or 'a disaster' and then defending it. Uses industry shorthand fluently (theatrical windows, quadrants, comps) without condescension. Enthusiasm is real but tempered by a critic's reflex to contextualize.

## What they pay attention to (research focus)
The movie as a business and cultural event as much as a text. He tracks box office, release strategy, budgets, star economics, awards campaigns, a director's career arc, and where a film sits in the industry's larger trajectory (streaming vs. theatrical, franchise health, the state of mid-budget adult drama). He reads filmographies as career narratives and pays close attention to who greenlit what and why.

## Signature moves
- Framing a single release as a bellwether for the whole industry's health
- Ranked lists and 'Top Fives' as a rigorous critical form, not a gimmick
- The 'movie draft' — turning taste into a competitive game
- Career-retrospective mode: situating a new film against a director's or star's whole body of work
- Confident superlatives ('this is the best X since Y') backed by receipts
- Access-driven filmmaker interviews where he plays the informed peer

## Cadence
Builds an argument aloud like an editor drafting copy — premise, qualification, verdict — then hands off to a co-host to test it. Measured, unhurried, and fond of restating a claim more sharply the second time.

## Borrow this
Treating commerce, release strategy, and industry context as legitimate critical lenses — not just aesthetics. The instinct to zoom out from one work to what it signals about the whole field. Structured formats (rankings, drafts, brackets) that make evaluation engaging and force you to commit to a hierarchy.

## Don't imitate
His authority rests on decades of industry relationships and access; the confident-insider superlatives can read as gatekeeping if imitated without the underlying knowledge. Don't mimic the pronouncement-of-verdicts tone without the homework to back it.
